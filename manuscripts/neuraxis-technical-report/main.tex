\title{Fast Software Control and Bounded Deliberation: Methods, Evidence, and Limits}
\author{Felipe Santibáñez-Leal}\date{}
\begin{document}\maketitle\headerblock
\begin{abstract}Neuraxis evaluates whether a software controller can combine fast admissible-action selection with selective delegation to slower planners. Twelve methods are compared across twenty software-operation families, six variants, and ten seeds, for 14,400 core episodes. Seven methods solve every core episode. The proposed resource-versioned outcome-aware controller M12 solves 1,160 episodes versus 1,200 for the guarded baseline, with substantially higher decision latency. Four component ablations match full M12 task success, and both fitted allocation gates select never-defer. A separate 288-episode structural-transfer lane records 24/24 successes for M04 and 21/24 for M12. A distinct 1,536-episode semantic-transfer lane records 38/128 for M09, 18/128 for M12, and 2/128 for M04; M12 loses 22 paired episodes and wins two against M09. The event-response analysis of 4,320 frozen episodes is exploratory and does not change the core result. The evidence supports neither an M12 advantage nor a measurable task-success contribution from its named components on these evaluated suites. The operational workbench uses a deterministic prerequisite policy and does not route tasks through M12. The work contributes an auditable software-action testbed and a falsifiable evaluation of fast and slow allocation, with negative findings about benchmark saturation and cancellation handling. It also documents every implemented method, data contract, evidence lane, and validation boundary for reproduction and extension.\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} software control, action brokerage, metacontrol, selective prediction, deliberation allocation, reproducible evaluation
\section{Introduction}
Software agents repeatedly choose small, consequential actions while the surrounding application changes. A build may finish between two observations; a user may cancel a running operation; an HTTP write may time out after the server committed it; a file may be replaced while a plan is pending. These are not merely language-generation problems. They combine choice, authority, resource identity, concurrency, and verification. A controller that emits a plausible next step can still act on stale evidence or mistake an attempted effect for a completed task.

Neuraxis makes this control problem inspectable. Its software-action runtime represents candidate operations explicitly, checks admissibility at the effect boundary, binds proposals to resource revisions, records effects, and evaluates task postconditions separately. The product couples that runtime to a research workbench containing twelve policy implementations and frozen experiment records. This report documents both layers: the application used for bounded file operations and the research comparison used to evaluate the policies. The distinction matters because the workbench task page uses a deterministic prerequisite selector; it does not silently execute the proposed metacontroller.

The motivating fast/slow question is narrower than a claim about two kinds of human thought. A controller can make a rapid proposal from local state, while a planner can spend more time to interpret ambiguity or search farther ahead. Which computation should control a particular decision depends on expected outcome change, latency, resource cost, uncertainty, and the authority boundary. A language model can serve either role according to how it is deployed; model family alone does not define a computational role. Biological findings motivate questions about arbitration and adaptive timescales, but they do not establish that a software component reproduces a neural mechanism \cite{daw2005,lee2014,kool2016,mante2013,dptagent}.

The report's contribution is an auditable comparison and its negative findings. The finite core suite is solved by several methods, including a very fast hand-authored controller. M12 is slower and fails on long-job cancellation, and its ablations do not change the aggregate success count. These outcomes are central to the account: they constrain the claim that can be made and motivate a better discriminating benchmark rather than a post-hoc success narrative. We ask whether the current data support a gain, explain the runtime and method boundaries, and identify the additional evidence needed to revisit the question.

\section{Problem formulation}
Interactive software agents select actions under changing state while respecting authority, time, and effect constraints. A language model can propose a sequence, but its context-dependent generation is costly and insufficiently constrained to act as the sole executor of every low-level operation. Neuraxis studies a computational division between a fast policy that chooses among typed candidates and a slower planner consulted when expected incremental utility justifies its cost. The system records arguments, resource versions, decisions, effects, effect records, and independently evaluated outcomes.

System One and System Two are analogies motivating a fast/slow allocation question. Neuraxis is not a model of biological cognition, and no neural equivalence is claimed. The empirical question is whether a metacontroller improves outcomes or cost over competent fixed, learned, and delegated policies while retaining a broker-enforced authority boundary. Results describe a finite authored software-operations suite, not arbitrary software or human behavior.

Formally, an episode is a sequence of observations and broker-mediated effects under a task specification. At time $t$, a policy receives observation $o_t$, history $h_t$, budget $B_t$, and a finite candidate set. Its decision may be a candidate, a planner request, or a typed stop. The broker decides admissibility from authority, current resource revisions, and the operation schema. The environment then produces an event and possibly an effect record. An evaluator computes success from a task postcondition after the episode completion condition. This decomposition defines the causal order that the event trace must preserve.

The comparison asks about two outcomes separately. Task utility is a postcondition result, while computational cost includes policy latency and planner work, including a completed answer that was discarded. For a paired decision, local delegation value is planner-branch verified utility minus measured cost and fast-branch utility. The estimate is identifiable only to the extent the branches share their declared initial state and continuation policy; unobserved operating-system state can still differ after reset. It is not a full-episode counterfactual. The experiment does not estimate a universal optimum; it tests implemented methods under a versioned protocol and reports measured bounds.

The authority constraint is hard rather than a reward preference. High expected utility cannot make a disallowed action eligible, and a planner answer cannot execute directly. This separation follows the principle that the controller proposes while a distinct runtime authorizes. It lets policy experiments vary independently from the effects contract, provided the same broker version is held constant across methods. If the broker or case specification changes, the comparison is a new experiment and must carry new lineage rather than being described as a seed variation \cite{hadfieldmenell2016,amodei2016,leike2018}.

\section{Validation}
Validation is performed at separate layers because no single green check establishes the whole product claim. The artifact checker verifies canonical episode hashes, matrix coverage, split identity, lineage, and recomputed outcomes. Specialized gates validate semantic-transfer, routing, native operation, event-response, readiness, and native export parity records. Contract tests check that declared producers, consumers, schema validators, and provider-swap boundaries exist. The notebook checker executes the 38 bilingual notebooks in clean kernels and checks their mirrored sequence and topic depth. Frontend gates cover type checking, tests, and production build; deployment validation separately checks the release manifest and live artifact identity. These gates answer different questions and are not interchangeable.

The scientific results remain bounded by the source artifacts. M12's 1,160 successes and M01's 1,200 are read from the audited aggregate derived from 14,400 hashed episodes. The benchmark adequacy record recomputes the 71 seed-variable cells and the 40 M12 cancellation failures. The semantic-transfer validator checks its 1,536 records separately and preserves the 128-case paired comparison. The event-response checker recomputes the 4,320-episode audit and exposes its post-hoc status. None of these validation passes converts a negative result to a positive one; they establish that the reported value matches the declared source and scope.

Runtime correctness requires additional fault-focused checks. Admission rejection, stale resource revision, duplicate event, unavailable provider, cancellation acknowledgement, journal recovery, and postcondition failure must each remain distinguishable. Export parity compares fitted Python and native outputs on the declared vector set; a small maximum numerical difference demonstrates implementation agreement only for that checkpoint and input set. It does not demonstrate calibration, policy quality, adversarial robustness, or safe effects in a new provider. Workspace service checks establish access control and bounded lifecycle under tested conditions, not a general security certification. The status of every validation boundary should therefore remain visible alongside the evidence it supports.

In the evaluation, multiple baselines achieve a ceiling and the proposed method does not. This is a validation finding about benchmark discrimination: a suite where a fixed rule controller solves every episode cannot identify an advantage from a more complex method on task success. The event-response analysis can help locate mismatches between response choice and final completion, but because it is post hoc it does not repair that identification problem. Algorithm~\\ref{alg:guarded} states the actual baseline order; Algorithms~\\ref{alg:async} and~\\ref{alg:m12} make the asynchronous and proposed method procedures reviewable against the implementation.
\section{Discussion}
The present evidence does not support the superiority prediction. M01 has higher success and much lower decision latency; M12 fails 40 cancellation episodes; all four ablations preserve its success count; the gate chooses never to defer; and the small transfer comparison favors XGBoost. The implementation exists, but the intended fast/slow allocation benefit is not demonstrated.

Typed contracts, broker authority checks, effect records, and recoverable journals provide an engineering substrate for evaluating different policies. These are runtime properties, not evidence for a cognitive architecture. Measure them through fault injection, stale-plan rejection, recovery correctness, and unauthorized-effect attempts, separately from task success. Event contracts and interruption-aware evaluation have also been applied to asynchronous-agent and stateful-navigation benchmarks; those studies motivate this additional diagnostic but do not validate the Neuraxis post-hoc analysis or establish performance on native software effects \cite{asyncbench,interruptbench}.
Figure~\ref{fig:native-export-parity} reports native export parity as a numerical check, not as outcome evidence. Figure~\ref{fig:routing-audit} shows the held-out initial-arm result and its explicitly post-hoc oracle ceiling. Native export parity is a separate numerical check. The vector audit reports maximum absolute probability error below the declared tolerance for its evaluated fitted methods; this establishes agreement on checked vectors and checkpoint, not outcome generalization, calibration, or safety. A separate 32-episode held-out initial-arm routing diagnostic records zero learned-gate delegations and zero successes, while always-M09 records ten verified successes with much higher latency. Its oracle ceiling also uses held-out event labels and is diagnostic only.

The results answer the implemented question negatively. A hand-authored controller solves all core cases at a fraction of the proposed controller latency; the learned deferral threshold chooses never-defer; and the named component ablations do not change M12 task success. This does not show that adaptive allocation is impossible. It shows that the current benchmark contains too little decision-relevant uncertainty and too many deterministic repeated cells to identify the expected gain. The semantic-transfer lane adds a different difficulty and favors direct deliberation for its measured split, while the structural lane favors boosted trees. Combining these lanes would hide rather than resolve the task-distribution dependence.

The runtime contribution and the method hypothesis should be judged separately. Typed candidate schemas, effect records, resource revision checks, and journal recovery make controller effects inspectable. These features offer an engineering boundary for experimenting with policies, but they are not evidence that M12 improves outcomes. Likewise, native parity shows that the checked Python and compiled outputs agree numerically; it is not an efficacy result. The product interface presents this distinction by separating operational work from benchmark evidence. The deterministic workbench policy is appropriate to its declared task graph but cannot be cited as an M12 deployment.

The literature gives useful design comparisons, not a shortcut to empirical validation. Arbitration between model-based and model-free value estimates motivates cost-sensitive policy selection; metareasoning formalizes the value of additional computation; contextual bandits and reinforcement learning supply alternative learners; selective prediction studies when to defer; and tool-use benchmarks expose stateful interaction demands. Recurrent neural dynamics and dual-process accounts offer analogies about timescales, while predictive-processing and hierarchy theories suggest testable hypotheses about surprise and persistence. None imply that a particular software architecture is biologically equivalent or that slow reasoning is categorically a language model. Neuraxis must establish each claim from measured software outcomes \cite{daw2005,lee2014,kool2016,russell1991,sutton1999,madras2018,mozannar2020,dptagent,toolsandbox,react,toolformer,swebench,taubench,rao1999,mante2013,botvinick,cho2014,li2010,schulman2017,suttonbarto2018,efron1993,demsar2006,nadeau2003,guo2017,friedman2001,chen2016,bai,jev,bs1,asyncbench,interruptbench,lamport1998,hadfieldmenell2016,amodei2016,leike2018}.

A more informative next experiment should register a decision-relevant change before test labels are opened: for example, tool behavior or authority changes during a pending request, paired with an independently verifiable planner benefit in a subset and planner harm in another. Families must be held out, seeds must vary environment transitions rather than relabel deterministic instances, and the fast baseline must remain competent. The outcome should include verified task success, effect safety, critical-path latency, total compute, planner utilization, stale-result rejection, and cancellation completion. The thresholds and analysis should be preregistered and the full failed denominator retained. This is a concrete path to falsification, not a promise that the proposed method will win.
\section{Limitations}
The core benchmark is saturated for success because a guarded finite-state controller solves it completely. A useful test of adaptive deliberation needs ambiguous or changing situations where fixed rules can fail and planning can resolve uncertainty. Training, tuning, and evaluation must use disjoint task families, with the held-out set inaccessible until evaluation.

Ten seeds provide little independent evidence when the simulator is deterministic. Future protocols should vary meaningful transitions, tool behavior, network conditions, and planner outcomes by seed, and quantify uncertainty across task families. Seven positive labels are insufficient for a robust deferral gate. Collect planner cases where a slow answer changes the outcome, as well as cases where delegation is wasteful or harmful. Separate calibration, threshold selection, and final evaluation.

Diagnose C07 cancellation through transition traces: request, acknowledgement, process termination, resource revision, and final postcondition. Evaluate any repair on an untouched cancellation family. Match compute budgets and report success, decision latency, total cost, effect safety, recovery, and planner use. A positive claim should require improvement over M01 and M04 with uncertainty reflecting independent families and no loss of authority guarantees.


The task suite is authored and finite. Its state flags and action vocabulary encode assumptions that align well with deterministic control. The guarded baseline reaches perfect core success, so the primary success outcome is saturated. The ten seed labels yield only 71 varying method-case-variant cells among 1,440. Consequently, nominal episode counts overstate independent variation, and confidence intervals derived from those repeats cannot support broad population claims. The small number of families is the appropriate limitation for external validity.

The learned gate has only seven positive incremental-utility labels in the fitting and calibration samples and selects threshold 1.01. Its never-defer behavior means the evaluation does not demonstrate learned planner selection. Branch reset shares declared inputs and history but cannot clone every hidden state of an operating system or external provider. Paired utility is therefore local to that design. Training budgets and model classes differ, and latency values depend on the recorded environment. The semantic-transfer sample has 128 matched cases per compared method and should be treated as descriptive evidence about this lane rather than a stable ranking for open-ended tools.

The cancellation audit identifies a mismatch between selected response and final goal completion, but does not isolate causal mechanism. It is post hoc, and its event definitions were authored after the original test episodes existed. The 4,320 episodes therefore do not provide confirmatory evidence. Similarly, vector parity is limited to the declared checkpoints and test vectors. Service and workspace checks cover their exercised paths but do not amount to a complete security assessment, provider certification, or production fault-injection study.

The proposed method integrates several components, so equal ablation success on this benchmark may reflect saturation rather than true irrelevance. Nevertheless, these results do not permit a positive component claim. A redesign must preserve the archived result and label new suites as new evidence. It must not overwrite frozen traces, silently change task contracts, or tune against final test outcomes. Any new performance claim requires a family-held-out test with meaningful stochastic transitions, adequate positive and negative delegation examples, matched total-cost accounting, and independently checked effect postconditions.

The core index contains 14,400 episodes. The separate event-response report binds 4,320 test episodes and records their source digests; its checker recomputes every score before release. Scientific artifact and notebook scripts support recomputation. Frozen traces enable deterministic inspection; fresh executions measure current runtime behavior and must be labeled separately. Experiment identifiers, package versions, and source lineage accompany the data.

The core index has 14,400 episodes; the event-response report binds 4,320 frozen episodes and its own source digest. Transfer indexes are separate. The checkers recompute outcomes and reject stale digest or denominator changes. Figure sources and notebook execution scripts regenerate visual and explanatory artifacts from repository evidence.

Core, structural-transfer, semantic-transfer, and event-response populations retain separate indexes and denominators.
\section{Conclusions}

Neuraxis provides a brokered software-action system, an operational workbench, and a versioned research comparison. The present evidence does not establish that the proposed fast/slow controller improves software-task outcomes. The fixed guarded baseline solves all core episodes at far lower decision latency; the proposed controller fails 40 cancellation cases, its ablations preserve the same success total, its fitted gate never delegates, and the distinct transfer results do not support a general advantage. These negative findings remain part of the product's research result.

The measured conclusion is methodological: typed authority, effect records, resource bindings and reproducible traces make the allocation hypothesis testable, while the current suite does not demonstrate its benefit. A family-held-out evaluation should vary task structure, resource changes, planner quality and effect outcomes independently, and report all costs and safety outcomes. Until those measurements exist, M12 is an implemented experimental controller whose performance hypothesis remains unsupported, not a validated improvement over simpler policies.
Neuraxis implements bounded software-action brokerage and evaluates M01 through M12 under a common task protocol. Current results do not show an advantage for M12: M01 solves every core episode at far lower decision latency, M12 fails 40 cancellations, ablations do not change success, and the separate structural and semantic transfer lanes do not support a general advantage. These negative findings motivate a variable, family-held-out evaluation. Fast and slow allocation remains a research question rather than an established capability. The separate resource-versioned metacontrol paper specifies M12 and its falsifiable allocation hypothesis, with the same core findings (Santibáñez-Leal, 2026).
